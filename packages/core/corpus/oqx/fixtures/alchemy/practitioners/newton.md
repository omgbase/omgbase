# Isaac Newton

1643–1727. Wrote more on alchemy than on physics, and published none of it.

known_for:: unpublished alchemical corpus
century:: 17

## Scale of the work

Roughly a million words of alchemical manuscript survive: transcriptions,
indexes of authors, and his own laboratory records with quantities and dates. He
kept furnaces at Trinity for decades and ran the work himself rather than
delegating it.

Keynes, who bought a large part of the papers at auction in 1936, called him
"the last of the magicians." The phrase is memorable and slightly unfair — the
notebooks are methodical, quantitative, and unmystical in tone.

## The index of authors

His *Index Chemicus* cross-references hundreds of alchemical texts by topic. It
is a serious piece of scholarship on a corpus he expected to contain a real
secret, encoded.

That expectation was the error, and it was not a careless one: the tradition
*claimed* to encode a secret, and Newton took the claim at face value and tested
it with the most rigorous method available. The method was sound; the corpus was
empty.

## Why it matters here

Newton is the clean case against reading alchemy as mere credulity. The most
capable investigator of the era worked the programme for thirty years with full
rigour and got nothing, because there was nothing there. That is a result, and
the [magnum opus](/processes/magnum-opus.md) framework does not survive it.
